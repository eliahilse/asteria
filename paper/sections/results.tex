\paragraph{Step 1: does context help at all?}
Round I07 (Table~\ref{tab:i07}) compares, in four cells, no security
context against three kinds written by a researcher: \emph{operations}
(what each step of the feature must do to stay safe), \emph{requirements}
(a constraint for each operation, with the operation named) and
\emph{boundaries} (where untrusted data enters and what to do there). All
twelve context arms fail fewer security checks than their control,
whichever way the unresolved checks are counted; the requirements insert
does best in every cell. Functionality is not traded away: the control
arms have 4 or 5 functional artifacts out of 5, the context arms likewise.
Reading all 79 delivered artifacts shows what the checks measure: control
artifacts do not ignore bad input, they normalise it, clamping a negative
time to zero or substituting a default name, and the contract counts that
as a failure because it asks for rejection. Insert wording maps onto code:
the only arm whose artifacts reject a negative score is the one whose
insert says ``reject negative scores''. Two checks never separate arms,
since every artifact survives a corrupted file and none deserializes Java
objects. Round I09 (Table~\ref{tab:i09}) asks how much of the effect is
knowledge of the code. A single generic sentence asking for a secure
implementation removes 23 of the control's 38 failed checks in Generation
and 15 of 31 in Reuse; the flat CWE Top~25 list, with no procedure behind
it, removes 13 and 18. The specific requirements insert removes the most,
26 and 30, but it was written by someone who knew the code.

\paragraph{Step 2: can an agent write it?}
The acquisition agent of Section~\ref{sec:method} produced six documents,
one per angle and task variant. Each finished within its budget, using 26 to 60 commands;
the harness kept 88 of the 90 statements produced and verified 342 of the 351 code locations they cite.
Table~\ref{tab:agent} follows the data-flow angle, which cites the most
code. Round I10 separates two things. Letting the model read the
repository on its own, without context, changes nothing: 32 and 35 failed
checks out of 50 against 33 and 35 for single-shot controls, at three to
five times the tool calls. The agent's full document as an insert, about
31,000 characters, gives 3 to 10 failed checks out of 50, the fewest so
far, but in Generation only 1 and 2 artifacts out of 5 are functional. The \emph{compact insert}, requirement and control
statements only, is the working form (rounds I16 and I16b): failed checks
fall from 29 to 2 in Reuse and from 37 to 3 in Generation, between 4.6 and
7.2 fewer per artifact, with no check getting worse. The price is
functionality: 3 functional artifacts out of 5 in each cell instead of 5,
and 18 submissions instead of 7 in Generation. So the agent's context is
stronger than the researcher's and costs something the researcher's did
not.

\paragraph{Step 3: timing or force?}
Before chasing the cost we tested delivery, with three trajectories per
arm. A \emph{sidecar} watches an agentic trajectory and either injects the
statements relevant to the code just read or edited, or asks a one-turn
judge whether a submission violates a cited control and then rejects it,
attaches advice, or rewinds the conversation two turns. Injecting on
demand changes when statements arrive, not how many, because the feature
touches the very methods they cite. Rejecting every flagged submission
produced no evaluable artifact; the judge itself was right on all 6 first
submissions it flagged. Advice keeps 3 of 3 artifacts functional but lowers
failed checks only from 19 to 16 out of 30; one rejection, or one rewind,
reaches 3 failed out of 30 with 0 of 3 functional. Only the compact insert
given before the first attempt reaches 3 failed out of 30 with functional
artifacts. When context arrives matters more than how hard it is enforced;
the rest of the study is single-shot.

\paragraph{Step 4: where the cost comes from.}
The functional tests include coupling tests that end a game run on the
test driver's player, whose name is null because it has no AI, and two of
them never set a name. The security contract asks the store to reject a
null name. An artifact passes both only if the code at the run-end hook,
the place where the game records a finished run, substitutes a name before
calling the store. We audited that hook in every artifact of rounds I09 to
I16b (Table~\ref{tab:hook}). All 48 control artifacts and all 30 artifacts
under researcher-written inserts substitute a name; the researcher's insert
said ``reject or safely normalize null names''. The agent's inserts said
something else at the hook: ``if the name is unavailable, skip recording''
in Generation and ``reject unavailable or inconsistent values'' in Reuse.
In 12 of 46 agent-insert artifacts the code does exactly that, the run is
not recorded, and precisely those two tests fail, with no other failure.
A second, independent model reader checked 12 classified rows and the
rule was refined until both agreed. The cost is one sentence the agent
wrote colliding with one property of the test fixture, not a loss of
capability.

\paragraph{Step 5: removing the sentence by rendering.}
The first route leaves the agent alone and renders its document without
the failure-behaviour lines (rounds I17 and I18). In Generation
this keeps the effect, 36 to 2 failed out of 50, and restores
functionality to 4 of 5 with no nameless run lost; in Reuse, where the
sentence sits inside the control statement rather than its failure line,
1 of 5 artifacts still loses the run, and one resource check gets worse.
Rendering cannot fix what the agent decided. The second route changes the
agent's instructions. Version~9 adds one rule: reject at the boundary where
untrusted data enters, substitute a default at the point where the feature
does its work, never skip the feature's effect. With fresh acquisitions
(round I19) no run is lost in either cell, but in Generation the agent
placed the substitution inside the store (``use the fallback Player at
storeRun''), the artifacts clamp and normalise inside the store as controls
do, and the effect almost vanishes: 37 to 31 failed out of 50. Reuse, whose
insert kept the store rejecting, goes from 35 to 9. The lesson is that the
effect of every earlier insert was carried by the word ``reject'' at the
store. Version~10 therefore binds the roles: the operation that receives
untrusted data rejects and never substitutes; the caller substitutes before
calling it. With fresh acquisitions (round I20) failed checks fall from 39
to 11 in Generation and from 31 to 7 in Reuse, between 4.4 and 5.8 fewer
per artifact; every artifact is functional in every arm; no run is lost;
and the insert arms need no more submissions than their controls (10 and
10; 11 and 13). Two more acquisitions per task variant, each against its own control
with three trajectories per arm (rounds I21a and I21b), show the same
direction in all four comparisons, 24 to 6 and 24 to 2 failed out of 30 in
Generation, 17 to 3 and 18 to 4 in Reuse, with every artifact functional:
the result belongs to the instructions, not to one lucky document. What
still fails are resource checks, and which ones varies by acquisition. One
moves the wrong way: under every version~10 Reuse insert, 2 or 3 artifacts
out of 3 to 5 keep more than one hundred records where all controls bound
them, and none of those inserts mentions a record limit while the earlier
insert did.

\paragraph{What this says.}
Repository-specific security context removes most of the security
failures a control produces, from about half with a generic sentence to
nine in ten with the best inserts, whether a researcher or an agent writes
it. An agent's context is stronger but every word of it is
followed literally, so a single unsafe sentence about failure behaviour
costs functionality, and the fix is a precise rule in the agent's
instructions about where rejection and where substitution belong.
Delivering the context before the first attempt beats policing the
attempts afterwards.

\paragraph{Limits.}
One task, one pair of games, one model as agent and generator. Each arm
shares one acquired document across its three to five trajectories; only
the final instructions were tried with three independent acquisitions.
Counts are descriptive. The large-record check is often unresolved (43 of
80 artifacts in round I07). Artifact reviews and hook classifications are
single-pass model judgements with cited lines, spot-checked by a second
reader on 12 rows.
