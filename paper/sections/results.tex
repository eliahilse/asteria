\paragraph{Step 1: does context help at all?}
Round I07 (Table~\ref{tab:i07}) compares, in four cells, no security
context against three kinds written by a researcher: \emph{operations}
(what each step of the feature must do to stay safe), \emph{requirements}
(a constraint on each operation, with the operation named) and
\emph{boundaries} (where untrusted data enters and what to do there). All
twelve context arms fail fewer security checks than their control, however
the unresolved checks are resolved; counting failures as observed, the
requirements insert has the fewest in every cell. Functional counts stay at
4 or 5 out of 5 in every arm; in three of the twelve context arms one
artifact fewer is functional than in the control. Reading all 79 delivered
artifacts (one trajectory delivered nothing) shows what the checks measure:
control artifacts normalise bad input, clamping a negative time to zero or
substituting a default name, and the contract counts that as a failure
because it asks for rejection. Insert wording
maps onto code: the only arm whose artifacts reject a negative score is the
one whose insert says ``reject negative scores''. In this round two checks
never separate arms, since every artifact survives a corrupted file and
none deserializes Java objects. Round I09 (Table~\ref{tab:i09}) asks how
much of the effect needs knowledge of the code. One generic sentence
removes 23 of the control's 38 failed checks in Generation and 15 of 31 in
Reuse; the CWE Top~25 list alone removes 13 and 18; the requirements insert
removes the most, 26 and 30, but it was written by someone who knew the
code.

\paragraph{Step 2: can an agent write it?}
The acquisition agent of Section~\ref{sec:method} produced six documents,
one per angle and task variant. Each finished within its budget, using 26 to 60 commands;
the harness kept 88 of the 90 statements produced and verified 342 of the 351 code locations they cite.
Table~\ref{tab:agent} follows the data-flow angle, whose documents cited
the most code; the other two angles were not taken into delivery rounds.
Round I10 makes two comparisons. Letting the model read the repository on
its own, without context, changes little: 32 failed checks out of 50 in
Generation and 35 in Reuse, against 33 and 35 for single-shot controls,
with three to five times as many tool calls per trajectory. The agent's
full document as an insert, about 31,000 characters per task variant, gives
5 and 3 failed checks out of 50 in Generation and 4 and 10 in Reuse, but in
Generation only 2 and 1 artifacts out of 5 are functional. The compact
insert keeps only the requirement and control statements (rounds I16 and
I16b): failed checks fall from 29 to 2 out of 50 in Reuse and from 37 to 3
in Generation, between 4.6 and 6.2 fewer per artifact in Reuse and between
5.8 and 7.2 in Generation, with no individual check getting worse. The
price is functionality: 3 functional artifacts out of 5 in each cell
instead of 5, and in Generation 18 submissions over the five trajectories
against 7 for the control. In the same cells the researcher's requirements insert had left 1 and
12 failed checks out of 50 (round I09) with every artifact functional.

\paragraph{Step 3: timing or force?}
Before diagnosing the cost we asked whether delivering the context
differently would avoid it (three trajectories per arm, Generation S). A \emph{sidecar} watches an agentic trajectory and either injects the
statements relevant to the code just read or edited, or asks a one-turn
judge (the same model, shown the edit and the statements) whether a
submission violates a cited control and then rejects it, attaches advice
to the functional feedback, or rewinds the conversation two turns.
Injecting on demand delivered the same statements as the static insert,
only later, because the feature touches the very methods they cite.
Rejecting every flagged submission produced no evaluable artifact. Run
silently, the judge flagged 6 first submissions and each of them failed at
least one security check in a category the cited statements named, so the
judge was right; rejection was the problem. Advice keeps 3 of 3 artifacts
functional but lowers failed checks only from 19 to 16 out of 30; one
rejection, or one rewind, reaches 3 failed out of 30 with 0 of 3
functional. Only the compact insert given before the first attempt reaches
3 failed out of 30 with 2 of 3 functional. In these pilots, context given
before the first attempt did better than any enforcement during the
attempts; the rest of the study is single-shot.

\paragraph{Step 4: where the cost comes from.}
The functional tests include integration tests that end a game run for a
player the test created without an AI, and in this game a player's name
comes from its AI, so that player has no name; two of these tests never set
one. The security contract asks the store to reject a missing name. An
artifact passes both only if the run-end method, the code that records a
finished run, supplies a name before calling the store. We audited that
method in every artifact of rounds I09 to I16b (Table~\ref{tab:hook}).
Every control artifact and every artifact under a researcher-written insert
records the nameless run, by substituting a default name or by a store that
accepts it; the researcher's insert said ``reject or safely normalize null
names''. The agent's inserts said something else at that method: ``if the
name is unavailable, skip recording'' in Generation and ``reject unavailable
or inconsistent values'' in Reuse. In 12 of 46 agent-context artifacts the
code does exactly that, the run is not recorded, and precisely those two
tests fail, with no other failure. Of the 19 agent-context artifacts that
are not functional, these 12 are explained by this sentence; the other 7
fail for other reasons, such as compile errors or a missing menu wiring. The
classification is a model judgement over cited lines; a second model
reader checked 12 rows, the rule was corrected where they disagreed and
reapplied to every row.

\paragraph{Step 5: removing the sentence by rendering.}
The first route leaves the agent alone and renders its document without
the failure-behaviour lines (rounds I17 and I18). In Generation this keeps
the effect, 36 to 2 failed out of 50, and restores functionality to 4 of 5
with no nameless run lost. In Reuse the sentence sits inside the control
statement rather than its failure line, so it survives the rendering: 1 of
5 artifacts still loses the run, and the huge-line check fails in 4 of 5
insert artifacts against 2 of 5 controls. Dropping the failure lines
removes the sentence only where it happens to live in one.

\paragraph{Step 6: removing it in the agent's instructions.}
Instruction version 9 adds one rule: reject at the boundary where untrusted
data enters, substitute a default at the point where the feature does its
work, never skip the feature's effect. With fresh acquisitions (round I19)
no run is lost in either cell, but in Generation the agent placed the
substitution inside the store (``use the fallback Player at storeRun''),
the artifacts clamp and normalise inside the store as controls do, and the
effect almost vanishes: 37 to 31 failed out of 50. Reuse, whose insert kept
the store rejecting, goes from 35 to 9. So the earlier effect depended on
the store rejecting rather than normalising, and a rule that lets the
substitution move into the store removes it. Version 10 therefore binds
the roles: the operation that receives untrusted data rejects and never
substitutes; the caller substitutes before calling it. With fresh
acquisitions (round I20) failed checks fall from 39 to 11 out of 50 in
Generation and from 31 to 7 in Reuse, between 4.6 and 5.8 fewer per
artifact in Generation and between 4.4 and 5.0 in Reuse; every artifact is
functional in every arm; no run is lost; and the insert arms need no more
submissions than their controls (Generation 10 against 10, Reuse 11
against 13, totals over five trajectories). Two more acquisitions per task
variant, each against its own control with three trajectories per arm
(rounds I21a and I21b), show the same direction in all four comparisons:
24 to 6 and 24 to 2 failed out of 30 in Generation, 17 to 3 and 18 to 4 in
Reuse, with every artifact functional. The direction and size do not
depend on one document. What still fails are resource checks, and which
ones varies by acquisition. One moves the wrong way: under the three
version 10 Reuse inserts, 3 of 5, 3 of 3 and 2 of 3 artifacts keep more
than one hundred records, where every control bounds them; none of those
three inserts mentions a record limit, while the earlier Reuse insert did.

\paragraph{Step 7: one response, no feedback.}
The prior study allowed one model response per prompt. Round I22 repeats the
final design (the I20 inserts, byte-identical) under that budget, five
trajectories per arm (Table~\ref{tab:agent}). Without a second submission
the delivery protocol itself becomes the first hurdle: 8 of the 20
responses were rejected before compilation, for listing the new class twice,
for an edit anchor that does not occur once in the file, or for a missing
menu edit; in the five-submission rounds the same slips are repaired on the
next attempt. Generation controls compile 5 of 5 and are functional 3 of 5;
with the insert 2 of 5 compile and 0 are functional. Reuse controls compile
1 of 5; with the insert 2 of 5, 1 functional in each. Where artifacts
compiled, the security effect is that of I20: the compiled insert artifacts
fail 3 of their 20 checks in each cell against 37 of 50 for the Generation
controls, and the identification bound in Generation is $-6.8$ to $-0.4$
failed checks per artifact; in Reuse, with 41 and 30 unresolved checks, no
direction can be stated. So under one response the insert costs delivery
and compilation before it can pay in security, a trade the five-submission
counts hide. The comparison with the prior study's compile rates remains
indirect: its pipeline repairs imports, packages and file placement before
compiling, which this harness does not.
